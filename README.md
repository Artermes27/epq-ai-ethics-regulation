# EPQ: Artificial Intelligence — Ethics & Regulation

**Extended Project Qualification (Level 3) · A-grade**

> *"Artificial Intelligence: the ethical challenges that it presents and how Governments have and should approach its regulation?"*

---

## Overview

An independent 5,000-word research project examining the nature of AI, the ethical concerns it raises for both consumers and developers, and how governments are responding through regulation. The project draws on primary sources including IBM, Google DeepMind, the UK AI White Paper, the EU AI Act, and the Bletchley Declaration.

This repository contains the full dissertation, the assessed presentation, and the AQA production log documenting the project lifecycle.

## Structure

### 1. What is AI?
Traces the history of computation from the Turing machine through to modern systems. Defines and distinguishes human intelligence from machine intelligence, and explains how software — particularly AI — closes that gap.

### 2. Categories of AI
Surveys the landscape from narrow reactive AI (e.g. IBM's Deep Blue) through to general AI and superintelligence. Establishes the capability envelope of each category as a foundation for assessing ethical risk.

### 3. Ethical Concerns
Analyses concerns from two perspectives:
- **Consumers** — bias and discrimination in automated decision-making, privacy erosion, deepfakes, lack of transparency, exposure of workers to harmful content
- **Developers** — risk of over-regulation stifling innovation; Goldman Sachs projection of 7% global economic growth attributable to AI over the next decade

### 4. Case Study — UK Regulation
Examines the UK's sector-specific, non-prescriptive approach: the House of Lords position that blanket AI regulation would be premature, the AI White Paper's five principles, and DSIT's responsible AI guidelines for recruitment. Argues that this approach correctly balances innovation with protection.

### 5. Case Study — EU AI Act
Analyses the EU's risk-tiered framework, which prohibits certain AI applications outright and imposes strict compliance requirements on high-risk systems. Evaluates both the protections it offers and the innovation concerns it raises.

### 6. Conclusion
Regulation is necessary to protect fundamental rights and to build the consumer trust that will ultimately drive AI adoption. The Bletchley Declaration's human-centric principle provides the right normative foundation. By acting now, governments can study the effects of early regulation and refine their approach iteratively.
